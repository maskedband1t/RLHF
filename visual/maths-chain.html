Bits and Belief

Nine ideas, in the order they depend on each other. Every one of them is an operation on a single object, and every one shows up inside a language model.

The object: a belief budget. 100 points to spread over what might happen. You must spend all of them.

One screen per idea. Each screen restates what it needs, so nothing assumes you still have the last one loaded.

01

The belief budget

in a language modelThe output at every single word position: one belief number for each of ~100,000 words in the vocabulary. Not a metaphor — literally the array the model emits.

100 squares. Each one is a point of belief you've assigned to an outcome. Drag the slider and watch what happens to the others.

The thing to notice

Belief is zero-sum. You cannot raise one without lowering the others. There is no "add more confidence" — only "move it from somewhere."

That single constraint is why training works the way it does. Push up the right answer and every wrong answer falls automatically. Nobody pushes them down by hand.

02

Softmax

in a language modelThe last operation in the network. The final layer emits raw scores; softmax turns them into next-word probabilities.

A model doesn't naturally emit numbers that sum to 1. It emits raw enthusiasm — logits — which can be any number at all. Softmax converts them in two steps.

That total is the only mystery worth clearing up. It is just the sum of the middle column. Every bar gets divided by the same number, which is what forces them to add to 1. Its own size means nothing.

The gap is the confidence

Exponentiating makes only the gaps matter. Scores 2,1,0 and 102,101,100 give the identical answer — try the slider at any lead and the shape only depends on the distance.

So adding to every logit does nothing at all. Multiplying them is a different story, and it has a name — next screen.

03

Temperature

in a language modelThe temperature knob in every API call. Same arithmetic: divide the logits by T before softmax.

One knob on the gaps, and nothing else. Divide the scores by T before softmax. Low T stretches the gaps apart. High T squashes them together.

What the knob really does

T below 1 makes the model bolder — it commits to its favourite. T above 1 makes it hedge — everything gets more equal. At T→0 it always picks the top word, every time.

Nothing about the model changed. You only rescaled the gaps it had already produced.

04

Entropy

in a language modelHow unsure it is about the next word. "United States of ___" → near zero. "My favourite ___" → high.

Surprise first, because entropy is built out of it. If something you gave little belief to happens, you're shocked. A lot of belief, barely shocked.

Entropy is that surprise averaged — weighted by your own beliefs. Not the worst case. What you'd pay on a typical day, if the world behaves the way you expect.

Two things that surprise people

Entropy is driven by the middle of your distribution, not the top. Near-certain outcomes happen constantly but carry no news. Near-impossible ones are shocking but almost never occur. The middling ones dominate.

And it takes one input: your beliefs. It cannot see what actually happened — there's no slot to put an outcome in. It's a self-assessment you can compute before reality arrives.

05

Cross-entropy

in a language modelThe pretraining loss itself. For every position in every document: raise belief on the word that actually came next.

Reality happens. You look up how much belief you'd placed on that outcome, and that's the bill. Tap an outcome to make it the one that happened.

L = − Σᵢ yᵢ · log qᵢ → − log q[what happened]

The truth is one-hot — [1,0,0] — so every term is multiplied by zero except one. The whole sum collapses to a single lookup. It's indexing wearing a summation's clothes.

The trap that cost you a prediction

The loss is the bill for what happened — not the penalty for being wrong. There is no notion of "wrong" anywhere in the formula. You pay every time, including when you're right. Being right just makes it cheap.

So "did the loss go up?" can't be answered until someone says what happened.

Also: cross-entropy and "the loss" are the same number. Not two layers. "Loss" is the job; cross-entropy is the thing doing it.

06

KL divergence

in a language modelThe leash in RLHF — how far the tuned model drifted from the base model. Also what DPO is algebraically derived from.

Two forecasters. Alice hedges 50/50. Bob is sure it'll be sunny. Pick a world and see what each one pays.

your bill = the floor + the waste KL is the waste

The floor is reality being genuinely unpredictable. Nobody beats it — not a genius, not a supercomputer. The waste is the only part that's your fault, and that part is KL.

Which explains two facts: KL is never negative (you can't waste less than nothing), and KL = 0 means your beliefs match reality — not that you pay nothing.

Why it isn't symmetric

Flip between the two worlds. Same two forecasters, and the waste is 2.33 versus 0.92 bits. "Distance" is the wrong word for KL.

Being overconfident in a random world is far worse than being over-hedged in a predictable one. Bob gets blindsided by rain fifty times. Alice just carries a pointless umbrella.

The rule: in KL(a‖b), the first slot says which world is real. The second says who's guessing.

07

Log-probs

in a language modelThe logprobs the API returns. A whole answer's score is the sum of its word log-probs.

A sentence is a chain of guesses, so its probability is all of them multiplied. Six words is fine. Five hundred is a catastrophe.

Sum of logs
—

Past about 237 words the multiplied value drops below the smallest number a computer can hold and silently becomes exactly 0.0. Not small — zero. And the slope of zero is zero, so every training nudge becomes zero. Learning stops dead, with no error message.

log(a × b) = log(a) + log(b)

That one school rule is doing all the work. Logs turn multiplying into adding. Multiplying drives you off a cliff; adding never falls off anything.

Reading the scale

A log-prob is not a different kind of probability — it's the same belief relabelled, like km and miles. And a negative log-prob is not a negative probability; logs of numbers under 1 are simply negative.

0 is perfect. −1 is a belief of 0.5. More negative is worse.

And you've met this quantity already: −log(p) is surprise — the exact thing you averaged to get entropy. One idea, three names, and papers use all three.

08

Policy gradient

in a language modelHow RLHF actually updates the weights. Sample an answer, score it, raise the log-probability of the words in answers that scored above average.

Three buttons paying 10, 9.5, 9. Slide the payouts up and down — it changes nothing about which button is best.

Left column is broken and the slider shows why. Every button gets shoved up — but belief is zero-sum, so "everything up" can't happen. What survives is whichever button got sampled most, and early on that's luck. It's learning from noise.

Right column subtracts the average first. Now the signal is signed: up, leave, down. And it's identical no matter where you slide the payouts, because it only ever sees differences.

θ ← θ + lr · advantage · ∇θ log πθ(action)

Read it as: nudge the numbers in whichever direction raises the log-probability of what I did, scaled by how much better than usual it turned out. That log is screen 07.

A correction kept on purpose

The first version of this claimed the baseline makes learning faster. The script said the opposite: raw rewards of ~10 act like a 20× bigger learning rate, so they move faster — sometimes confidently to the wrong button, on 6 of 12 random seeds.

It isn't a speed trick. It buys invariance and lower variance. "Faster" and "better" are different axes.

"Better than usual" is a real signal. "Good" is not. One subtraction, three names: baseline, advantage, A = r − V(s). GRPO is just using a group of samples' own mean as that baseline — which is why it needs no value network.

09

Calibration

in a language modelWhen it says 90%, is it right 9 times in 10? The hallucination question — and the hand-off question.

Entropy only looks at the model's beliefs. The bill only arrives after reality does. They come apart exactly when the model is confidently wrong.

A model below the line is overconfident — the dangerous direction. It says 95% and is right 70% of the time. Its entropy is low, so by its own reckoning it's doing great.

Why this is the one that matters to you

A hand-off decision isn't dangerous when the model is uncertain — uncertainty is visible and you can act on it. It's dangerous when the model is certain and wrong.

Entropy cannot detect that. It has one input and reality isn't it. Only comparing stated confidence against measured outcomes can — which is this chart, and nothing else.
